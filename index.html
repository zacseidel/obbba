How much did the One Big Beautiful Bill change your taxes?

When President Trump took office in January 2025, many provisions of the 2017 tax cuts were set to expire at the end of the year, which would have raised taxes for most Americans. He proposed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Congress passed it, and he signed it into law on July 4, 2025. Below you can see what it means for your taxes.

Compare your 2026 federal income tax under the law that passed with what you would owe if Congress had done nothing and the 2017 tax cuts had expired.

How this works

The three versions of 2026 tax law. "No bill passed" means the individual provisions of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act expire on schedule. That brings back the pre-2018 brackets (top rate 39.6%), personal exemptions, a smaller standard deduction, a $1,000 Child Tax Credit, unlimited SALT deductions with the old Alternative Minimum Tax, and no 20% business income deduction. "2025 rules continue" keeps the TCJA exactly as it was in 2025, adjusted for inflation, without anything the OBBBA added. "OBBBA" is the law as signed on July 4, 2025, using the IRS's 2026 inflation adjustments.

Households like yours. The page finds about 80 families in the 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances with the same marital status and housing, similar age, and the closest wages (or, if you choose Retirement, the closest retirement income). Your wages or retirement income are used as entered. Each family's other income is scaled by the ratio of your main income to theirs, and its home value and mortgage are used as reported. Anything you type in replaces the survey value for every family. The percentile compares total household income with all U.S. households in the survey. Your children, ages and state are then applied, and each family's taxes are computed under each version of the law. The range shows how much results depend on details you didn't enter. Each family counts according to its survey weight, capped at four times the group median so that no single family dominates. Higher-income families whose income is mostly unexplained "other income" are left out.

Simplifications.

  • Federal income tax only, including the 3.8% net investment income tax. Payroll taxes are not affected by the OBBBA and are left out. So are the OBBBA's changes to Medicaid, SNAP and ACA eligibility, and the separate expiration of enhanced ACA premium credits.
  • State income tax uses 2026 rates, brackets and deductions from the Tax Foundation, plus average local income tax rates where they exist. It is held the same across scenarios and matters here only through the SALT deduction. Property tax is your home value times your state's average effective rate (2024 American Community Survey, via the Tax Foundation).
  • Mortgage interest is estimated at 4.3% of the survey family's mortgage balance. The survey doesn't record charitable giving, so it defaults to 2% of income.
  • Interest, tax-exempt interest, dividends, capital gains and Social Security come from separate questions in the full SCF public file. Tax-exempt interest is left out of taxable income but counts in the Social Security taxability test. 80% of dividends are treated as qualified, close to the IRS national average. Anything the survey records as pension income or retirement-account withdrawals is treated as fully taxable. Business income is assumed to qualify fully for the 20% QBI deduction (the wage and specialized-service limits are ignored).
  • Pre-2018 dollar amounts are carried forward to 2026 with chained CPI. The parameters for the two counterfactuals are estimates, not published IRS figures.
  • The OBBBA's deductions for tips, overtime, car loan interest and seniors (Schedule 1-A) end after 2028. The higher SALT cap falls back to $10,000 in 2030. These results describe 2026 only.
  • Every household is assumed to meet the new Social Security number requirements. Dependent care credits, education credits and other smaller provisions are not modeled.

Sources. Federal Reserve, 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances (summary extract, the five implicates averaged per family) · IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (2026 parameters), via the Tax Foundation · Tax Foundation, 2026 state income tax rates · Tax Foundation, property taxes by state.

This is an estimate for illustration, not tax advice.
